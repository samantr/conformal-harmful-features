# Abstract

Adaptive conformal prediction sets are useful only when they are both valid and informative, yet conventional feature-selection objectives need not align with prediction-set efficiency. We study whether feature removal can be guided directly by its effect on adaptive conformal set size. We introduce **conformal harm**, a retraining-based feature-selection criterion that evaluates candidate removals using cross-fitted tuning-only evidence while enforcing prespecified safeguards on classification-accuracy loss and empirical marginal-coverage shortfall. One-shot and recursive removal paths are frozen before fresh conformal calibration and held-out test evaluation.

We evaluate logistic regression and a small neural network on Dry Bean, Covertype, and Human Activity Recognition (HAR), using APS as the primary score and a frozen robustness grid spanning RAPS, Base, ordinary temperature scaling, and Conformal Temperature Scaling. The final design contains 20 paired seeds for Dry Bean, 10 for Covertype, and 20 for HAR. Conformal-harm removals often differed from MI, permutation importance, RFE, SHAP, and CRFE, although exact feature identities were not consistently stable across seeds. The strongest result occurred for the Dry Bean small neural network: one-shot and recursive selection reduced mean Base-APS set size by 0.01697 and 0.01776 labels, respectively, while increasing mean accuracy by 0.00328 and 0.00368; both paths also outperformed all five matched deterministic selectors on set size after Holm correction. HAR small-NN selection produced reproducible size reductions of 0.01777 and 0.01322, but empirical coverage remained below the nominal 0.90 target. Under ConfTS, a residual one-shot feature benefit remained on HAR, but the joint feature-plus-scaling effect was sub-additive rather than synergistic, and numerical-boundary flags were substantial. Subject-level HAR analysis reproduced efficiency gains but did not establish coverage or conditional-reliability improvement.

These results show that conformal efficiency can define a feature-selection objective distinct from conventional predictive importance, but its benefit is model- and dataset-dependent and must be interpreted jointly with coverage, reliability, numerical behavior, and feature-selection stability.
