# 1 Introduction

Modern classifiers are increasingly expected to communicate uncertainty rather than return a single label without qualification. Conformal prediction provides a model-agnostic route to set-valued predictions with finite-sample marginal coverage guarantees under the relevant exchangeability conditions [@shafer2008tutorial]. In multiclass problems, however, validity is only one part of usefulness. A prediction set that contains many labels can satisfy a coverage target while offering little practical discrimination. Adaptive Prediction Sets (APS) and Regularized Adaptive Prediction Sets (RAPS) address this problem by constructing prediction sets from the classifier's ranked probability profile, making **prediction-set efficiency**—typically measured by set size—a central objective alongside coverage [@romano2020adaptive; @angelopoulos2021raps].

The probability representation delivered to a conformal predictor matters for this efficiency objective. Temperature scaling is highly effective as a conventional post-hoc confidence-calibration procedure [@guo2017calibration], yet better probability calibration does not necessarily imply smaller adaptive conformal sets. Xi et al. showed that ordinary confidence calibration can enlarge adaptive prediction sets and introduced Conformal Temperature Scaling (ConfTS), which tunes temperature directly for conformal efficiency [@xi2025confts]. Other work has optimized conformal set size by modifying model training [@stutz2022conftr], adapting trained logits [@liu2025cadapter], or explicitly separating efficiency-oriented tuning from fresh conformal calibration [@wu2026dco]. Collectively, these results suggest that the design choices surrounding a classifier—not merely the final conformal quantile—can materially influence the informativeness of its prediction sets.

Feature selection is one such design choice. Conventional feature-selection and feature-importance methods usually target predictive performance, parsimony, computational efficiency, or interpretability [@guyon2003feature]. RFE uses model-based relevance to eliminate features recursively [@guyon2002rfe]; SHAP explains model outputs through Shapley-value attributions [@lundberg2017shap]; and permutation-based importance measures a model's predictive reliance on a feature [@fisher2019reliance]. These objectives need not coincide with the contribution of a feature to the size of a calibrated adaptive prediction set. A variable can be predictive while also changing the probability geometry in a way that makes conformal sets larger, and removing a variable can affect classification accuracy, confidence structure, calibration, and conformal efficiency differently. This motivates a distinct question: **can a feature be harmful to conformal efficiency even when conventional importance measures do not identify it as dispensable?**

Conformal prediction has previously been used for feature selection, so this question does not arise in an empty literature. Early work proposed strangeness-minimization feature selection for confidence machines and feature selection based on conformal confidence [@bellotti2006smfs; @yang2011conformalfs], while CFSWIT used conformal wrappers for joint feature and instance selection in transfer settings [@zhou2018cfswit]. Most directly, Conformal Recursive Feature Elimination (CRFE) recursively removes features that increase global nonconformity under additively decomposable nonconformity functions and evaluates the resulting prediction-set efficiency and subset stability [@lopezdecastro2026crfe]. These methods establish conformal feature selection and recursive conformal elimination as prior concepts. The open question addressed here is narrower: whether the effect of **retraining after feature removal on modern APS/RAPS efficiency** can itself serve as the selection signal when accuracy and empirical coverage are explicitly safeguarded, and whether any resulting benefit is robust to model family, dataset, probability scaling, and repeated data splits.

We operationalize this idea through **conformal harm**. For a candidate feature removal, a classifier is refit and evaluated using cross-fitted evidence confined to an outer tuning partition. The removal is eligible only when the estimated accuracy loss and marginal-coverage shortfall remain within prespecified limits; among eligible candidates, a reduction in mean prediction-set size defines the efficiency signal. This makes harmfulness a property of a feature--model--pipeline combination rather than an intrinsic label attached to a variable. We study both a one-shot path, which freezes the initial single-feature order, and a recursive path, which re-estimates candidate removals after each step. The all-feature model is always an admissible step, so the procedure can decline to remove features when tuning evidence does not support an intervention. After selection, the subset is frozen, the classifier is refit, a fresh outer conformal-calibration split supplies the final threshold, and a held-out test split is used once for evaluation.

The study is organized around four hypotheses. **H1** asks whether features selected as conformally harmful differ from those favored by conventional feature-selection or importance procedures. **H2** asks whether removing the selected features improves conformal efficiency while retaining acceptable predictive and empirical-coverage behavior. **H3** asks whether feature removal provides benefit beyond probability scaling and whether their joint effect is complementary or synergistic. **H4** asks whether any efficiency gain is accompanied by improved reliability under subject-disjoint evaluation, using Human Activity Recognition (HAR) as the grouped-data setting. These hypotheses deliberately separate selected-feature identity, efficiency, scaling interaction, and reliability; success on one dimension is not taken as evidence for the others.

The experimental design is constructed to make those distinctions auditable. We evaluate logistic regression and a small neural network on Dry Bean, Covertype, and HAR, using four-way train/tune/calibration/test separation and subject-disjoint splitting for HAR. Proposed subsets are compared with mutual information, permutation importance, RFE, SHAP, CRFE, matched random subsets, and the all-feature reference. Robustness is evaluated over APS and a prespecified RAPS grid, two alpha levels, Base/ordinary temperature scaling/ConfTS, multiple accuracy-loss allowances, fixed removal counts, and paired seeds. The final design contains 20 paired seeds for Dry Bean, 10 for Covertype, and 20 for HAR, with the 20-seed extensions triggered by a prespecified precision rule rather than significance. HAR is additionally evaluated at the held-out-subject level with a two-way seed/subject bootstrap. Numerical exact-boundary events are retained and flagged rather than filtered after observing test behavior.

This protocol supports a more qualified conclusion than a universal feature-selection claim. The verified evidence supports **H1 with qualification**: conformal-harm removals often disagree with conventional selectors, but exact harmful-feature identities are not stable across seeds in all settings. **H2 is partially supported overall**, with the strongest evidence for the small neural network on Dry Bean; HAR also exhibits reproducible small-NN efficiency gains, but empirical coverage is below the nominal 0.90 target, so these gains cannot be described as coverage-preserving conformal improvements. **H3 is partially supported descriptively**: feature removal can retain a residual benefit under ConfTS, especially for HAR small NN, but the combined effect is sub-additive rather than general synergy. **H4 is not generally supported**: subject-weighted HAR efficiency effects are reproducible for the small neural network, whereas coverage and conditional-reliability improvements are not established. These outcomes make model and dataset dependence part of the scientific result rather than a nuisance to be averaged away.

The resulting contribution is therefore twofold. Methodologically, we formulate an efficiency-oriented, retraining-based feature-removal criterion for adaptive conformal classification with explicit tuning-only accuracy and coverage safeguards, together with one-shot and recursive selection procedures that preserve an untouched final calibration/test evaluation. Empirically, we provide a robustness study that separates aggregate efficiency from feature-identity stability, compares the method against conventional and conformal feature selectors at matched subset sizes, examines interaction with probability scaling, and exposes grouped-data and numerical limitations rather than treating smaller sets as sufficient evidence of improvement. The central question is not whether fewer features are universally better, but when feature removal changes a conformal predictor in a way that is both more efficient and scientifically defensible.

The remainder of the paper first reviews adaptive conformal prediction, conformal-efficiency optimization, feature selection, and stability. We then define conformal harm and the progressive selection procedures, describe the frozen experimental and statistical protocol, report the verified multi-seed results, and discuss the conditions under which efficiency gains can—and cannot—be interpreted as valid conformal improvements.
