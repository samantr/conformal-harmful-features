# 2 Background and Related Work

## 2.1 Conformal classification and prediction-set efficiency

Conformal prediction constructs set-valued predictions whose marginal coverage can be controlled under the relevant exchangeability assumptions without requiring a correct parametric model [@shafer2008tutorial]. In multiclass classification, this guarantee alone is not sufficient for an informative predictor: a method that routinely returns many or all labels can satisfy marginal coverage while providing little discrimination. Prediction-set **efficiency**, commonly summarized by set size, is therefore a central secondary objective.

Adaptive Prediction Sets (APS) address heterogeneity in classification difficulty by using the cumulative probability mass of labels ranked ahead of a candidate label in the conformity score [@romano2020adaptive]. This makes the number of labels included responsive to the classifier's probability profile rather than imposing a fixed set size. Regularized Adaptive Prediction Sets (RAPS) add a rank-dependent penalty to discourage unnecessary low-probability labels, which can substantially improve efficiency in problems with many classes [@angelopoulos2021raps]. The present study uses APS as the primary selection setting and retains RAPS as a prespecified robustness dimension. Accordingly, “improvement” is not defined by classification accuracy alone: changes in prediction-set size must be interpreted together with empirical coverage and conditional-reliability diagnostics.

The distinction between marginal and conditional behavior is also important. Classical split-conformal guarantees concern marginal coverage under exchangeability, whereas realized coverage can vary across classes, subpopulations, or shifted environments. Work on conformal prediction beyond exchangeability formalizes why the usual guarantee can degrade when exchangeability is violated and develops weighted/randomized remedies for nonexchangeable data [@barber2023beyond]. More recent group-conditional formulations, such as Kandinsky conformal prediction, target richer coverage behavior across possibly overlapping groups [@bairaktari2025kandinsky]. These developments motivate our separate reporting of marginal coverage, size-stratified coverage violation, class-coverage gaps, and subject-level HAR effects. They also motivate a strict distinction between a subject-disjoint experimental split and a proof of conformal validity under cross-subject shift.

## 2.2 Calibration, conformal optimization, and efficiency-aware adaptation

Probability calibration and conformal efficiency are related but different objectives. Temperature scaling is a widely used post-hoc calibration method that selects a scalar temperature on held-out data, typically by minimizing negative log-likelihood [@guo2017calibration]. For adaptive conformal scores, however, improving probability calibration need not reduce prediction-set size. Xi et al. showed that standard confidence-calibration procedures can enlarge adaptive conformal prediction sets and that smaller temperatures can sometimes improve efficiency; they proposed Conformal Temperature Scaling (ConfTS), which chooses the temperature using a conformal-efficiency-oriented loss rather than conventional calibration loss [@xi2025confts]. This observation is directly relevant to the present study, which treats Base, ordinary TS, and ConfTS as distinct experimental conditions rather than assuming that better confidence calibration is automatically better for conformal prediction.

Efficiency has also been optimized earlier in the learning pipeline. Conformal Training (ConfTr) differentiates through the conformal procedure so that the classifier itself is trained toward smaller prediction sets [@stutz2022conftr]. C-Adapter instead learns an order-preserving transformation of model outputs, seeking to improve conformal efficiency while retaining the classifier's label ranking [@liu2025cadapter]. These approaches demonstrate that conformal efficiency can be influenced by model fitting and output adaptation, not only by the choice of nonconformity score.

A closely related design issue is the separation of optimization data from final calibration data. Decoupled Conformal Optimisation (DCO) explicitly advocates a train--tune--calibrate design in which structural choices are made on an independent tuning split and a fresh calibration split is reserved for the final conformal quantile [@wu2026dco]. Our outer train/tune/calibration/test separation is consistent with the same broad statistical principle. The contribution claimed here is therefore not the act of decoupling tuning and calibration. Rather, the relevant question is whether **feature removal and retraining** can serve as an efficiency-oriented structural intervention when subset choice is made from tuning-only evidence and then frozen before fresh conformal calibration.

These lines of work also clarify why feature selection and scaling should not be treated as interchangeable. Feature removal changes the fitted predictive representation; temperature scaling changes the geometry of the output probabilities while leaving the underlying class ranking unchanged; conformal-score regularization changes the score itself. Their effects can therefore overlap, reinforce one another, or counteract one another. This motivates the explicit interaction analysis in this paper, while the frozen interpretation remains conservative: a residual benefit under ConfTS is evidence of complementarity only if it survives the scaling intervention, and an effect smaller than the sum of separate gains is sub-additive rather than synergistic.

## 2.3 Feature selection, feature importance, and conformal feature selection

Conventional feature selection serves several goals, including predictive performance, parsimony, computational efficiency, and interpretability [@guyon2003feature]. Different selectors operationalize relevance differently. Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE), for example, repeatedly removes variables using a fitted model's relevance criterion [@guyon2002rfe]. SHAP attributes model outputs to features using a Shapley-value framework [@lundberg2017shap]. Permutation-based importance measures how predictive performance changes when a feature is disrupted; importantly, variable importance can vary across different well-performing models, so it is not an intrinsic property of the feature alone [@fisher2019reliance]. Mutual-information filters similarly target association with the response rather than the downstream behavior of a conformal prediction set.

The distinction in this paper is therefore one of **estimand**, not a claim that standard importance methods are deficient. A feature may be useful for point prediction yet have little effect on adaptive-set size after calibration; conversely, removing a feature may improve the probability geometry relevant to a conformal score without making that feature conventionally “unimportant.” The proposed conformal-harm criterion directly estimates the change in prediction-set efficiency after retraining, while constraining estimated accuracy loss and marginal-coverage shortfall. This is why disagreement with MI, permutation importance, RFE, or SHAP is scientifically meaningful, but such disagreement does not establish that one method has discovered a uniquely correct feature identity.

Feature selection within conformal prediction has a substantial history and predates the present study. Bellotti, Luo, and Gammerman proposed Strangeness Minimisation Feature Selection for confidence machines, selecting features using aggregate nonconformity information [@bellotti2006smfs]. Yang et al. subsequently used conformal confidence to determine how many features were sufficient to reach a desired average confidence level [@yang2011conformalfs]. The conformal-prediction monograph later reviewed feature-selection procedures and emphasized issues such as selection bias and exchangeability [@bellotti2014feature]. Zhou et al. proposed CFSWIT, a conformal wrapper that jointly selects features and relevant source instances for instance-transfer problems [@zhou2018cfswit]. These methods rule out any broad claim that conformal prediction and feature selection have not previously been combined.

The closest methodological comparator is Conformal Recursive Feature Elimination (CRFE) [@lopezdecastro2026crfe]. CRFE adapts the RFE paradigm by decomposing an additive nonconformity function into feature contributions, recursively removing the feature that most increases global nonconformity, refitting after elimination, and optionally using a geometric stopping criterion. Its empirical evaluation explicitly considers prediction-set efficiency and subset stability. Thus, recursive conformal feature elimination and efficiency-aware conformal feature selection are both prior concepts.

Our method differs from CRFE in how harmfulness is operationalized and constrained. Rather than deriving per-feature relevance from an additive decomposition of a nonconformity criterion within CRFE's model-dependent framework, we evaluate retrained ablations through modern adaptive conformal prediction. Candidate removals are assessed directly by their estimated reduction in APS set size, with explicit tuning-only gates on classification-accuracy loss and one-sided marginal-coverage shortfall; conditional-reliability quantities are retained as additional diagnostics. The framework is applied to both logistic regression and a small neural network, and both fixed one-shot and recursively re-estimated removal paths are evaluated. These are design differences, not claims of historical priority or guaranteed superiority.

## 2.4 Stability, grouped robustness, and the scope of attribution

A useful feature selector should be evaluated not only by downstream predictive performance but also by the stability of its feature preferences. Nogueira et al. define feature-selection stability as robustness of preferences to data sampling and algorithmic variation and provide a statistical framework for evaluating stability measures [@nogueira2018stability]. Kuncheva's chance-corrected index is a widely used subset-overlap measure for fixed-size selections [@kuncheva2007stability]. López-De-Castro et al. have additionally proposed conformal prediction intervals for estimating feature-selection stability when only a limited number of selected subsets are available [@lopezdecastro2024conformalstability].

These studies motivate an important separation in our analysis. A method may yield a reproducible **aggregate performance effect** while selecting different but functionally substitutable features in different seeds; conversely, stable feature identities need not imply improved conformal efficiency. We therefore report within-seed overlap between conformal-harm removals and conventional selectors separately from across-seed top-k stability. Complete conventional rankings can be summarized with rank correlation as well as overlap, whereas a finite recursive removal path is only a partial ranking and should not be assigned a fabricated complete-rank correlation.

Grouped HAR evaluation introduces a second notion of robustness. Repeated activity windows from the same participant are dependent, and subject-disjoint partitioning prevents direct subject leakage but does not establish exchangeability between calibration and held-out subjects. In this setting, smaller subject-weighted prediction sets constitute an efficiency result only if they are described separately from nominal conformal validity. Our two-way seed/subject bootstrap is therefore used to quantify repeatability of observed effects over the sampled seed/subject structure, not to manufacture additional independent replicates or to claim a group-conditional coverage guarantee.

Taken together, prior work suggests that conformal efficiency can be changed through score construction, model training, output scaling, adapters, and feature selection. The present paper studies a narrower question: whether retraining after removing features identified by tuning-only **conformal-efficiency harm** can improve adaptive prediction sets under explicit predictive and empirical-coverage safeguards, and how robust that effect remains across models, datasets, conventional selectors, probability scaling, random seeds, and grouped HAR evaluation. The literature therefore motivates a bounded contribution rather than a first-of-its-kind claim.
