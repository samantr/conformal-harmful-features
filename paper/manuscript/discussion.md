# 6 Discussion

## 6.1 What constitutes a conformally harmful feature

The central premise of this study is that feature value for classification and feature value for adaptive conformal prediction need not coincide. The verified H1 results support this premise, but only in a qualified sense. The features removed by the proposed conformal-harm procedure were often different from those removed by mutual information, permutation importance, RFE, SHAP, and the matched CRFE baseline. The distinction was especially pronounced for the small neural network on HAR, where the proposed one-shot removals had zero within-seed Jaccard overlap with all five matched selectors, while Dry Bean showed low but nonzero overlap. These findings establish disagreement in selected removals at matched removal counts; they do not establish that the proposed method recovers a unique or universally correct set of intrinsically harmful features.

This distinction is conceptually important because conventional feature importance and conformal efficiency address different objectives. Mutual information, permutation importance, RFE, and SHAP are primarily tied to predictive association, prediction sensitivity, or changes in a model-level performance criterion. By contrast, the present method asks whether removing a feature can improve the efficiency of APS/RAPS prediction sets subject to explicit constraints on predictive performance and coverage-related behavior. A feature may therefore contribute little to classification error while still altering the probability ordering, concentration, or conformal score distribution in a way that changes prediction-set size. The current experiments demonstrate the empirical possibility of this divergence, but they do not isolate a single causal mechanism through which particular features change conformal scores.

The across-seed results further limit how the term “conformally harmful feature” should be interpreted. Aggregate efficiency effects can be repeatable even when the identity of the first removed feature is not. For the proposed small-NN paths, top-1 feature agreement across seeds was weak on Dry Bean and absent on Covertype and HAR. Some logistic-regression paths were more stable, showing that instability is itself model- and dataset-dependent. Because the pairwise seed comparisons overlap, they should not be read as independent replications, and because the progressive paths are partial, no complete-rank correlation can be inferred. Thus, H1 supports selection distinctness rather than a claim of seed-invariant harmful-feature identities. Correlated or redundant predictors could plausibly permit multiple effective removals, but that explanation was not directly tested here and should remain a hypothesis rather than a conclusion.

Operationally, the most defensible interpretation is therefore contextual: a feature is conformally harmful relative to a specified model, dataset, conformal score, scaling regime, and accuracy/coverage constraint when its removal improves the targeted conformal efficiency criterion without violating the prespecified selection rules. This definition is narrower than generic feature importance and is consistent with the observed instability of exact feature identities. It also avoids turning a useful selection diagnostic into an intrinsic property of a feature independent of the learning system in which it is evaluated.

## 6.2 When harmful-feature removal improves conformal efficiency

H2 is partially supported overall, with a clear dependence on both model family and dataset. The strongest evidence comes from the small neural network on Dry Bean. Under the primary Base-APS setting, both one-shot and recursive removal reduced mean prediction-set size by approximately 0.017 labels, and both effects were supported by the frozen paired inference. In the same setting, mean classification accuracy increased rather than decreased. Observed coverage remained close to the nominal 0.90 target in aggregate, although it was not uniformly at or above 0.90 across conditions or seeds. This combination makes Dry Bean small NN the clearest example in which the proposed selection improved efficiency without an observed accuracy trade-off and without the pronounced coverage deficit seen on HAR.

The HAR small-NN results are informative for a different reason. Feature removal produced reproducible reductions in prediction-set size in both the seed-level primary analysis and the subject-weighted analysis, but empirical coverage was below the nominal 0.90 target before and after removal. The efficiency signal is therefore real within the frozen evaluation, yet it cannot be described as a coverage-preserving conformal improvement. This distinction is essential: smaller sets are useful only when interpreted together with validity and reliability, and the HAR results show that an efficiency gain can coexist with a validity problem that feature selection does not repair. The same caution applies to accuracy. Mean HAR small-NN accuracy changes were positive, but their intervals crossed zero; absence of a significant accuracy loss is not a noninferiority or equivalence result.

Evidence for logistic regression and Covertype was substantially weaker. The primary logistic-regression effects were small, null, or slightly adverse, and neither model on Covertype established a reliable primary efficiency gain after correction. These results argue against viewing conformal-harm removal as a generic preprocessing step that should help any classifier. Instead, they support a conditional claim: efficiency-oriented feature removal can be beneficial in some model–dataset combinations, but its utility must be established for the operating regime under study.

The model dependence also cautions against inferring a neural-network-specific mechanism from significance patterns alone. The small neural network showed the clearest gains, but the experiments did not include a formal model-by-selection interaction test, and different corrected significance decisions across model families are not themselves evidence of such an interaction. A mechanistic explanation would require additional measurements of how removal changes logits, probability concentration, rank structure, or conformal-score geometry. The present results establish heterogeneity of effects, not its cause.

## 6.3 Comparison with conventional feature-selection baselines

The matched-baseline analysis strengthens the Dry Bean finding because it asks a more demanding question than comparison with the all-feature model alone: at the same number of removed features, does the proposed selection produce more efficient conformal sets than alternative selectors? For Dry Bean small NN, both one-shot and recursive paths produced smaller Base-APS sets than all five prespecified matched selectors—MI, permutation importance, RFE, SHAP, and CRFE—under the supplied Holm-adjusted comparisons. This is the strongest evidence that the gain is linked to *which* features were removed rather than merely to reducing dimensionality by a given amount.

HAR again shows a qualified pattern. The small-NN one-shot path outperformed four of the five matched selectors on set size after correction, whereas the recursive path outperformed only SHAP at the 0.05 level. These contrasts demonstrate that the proposed criterion can identify removals that are competitive with or better than conventional choices in some HAR settings, but they do not support universal selector superiority. Moreover, because HAR coverage is below nominal, even a matched set-size advantage should not be converted into a claim of a better valid conformal predictor without the corresponding validity qualification.

The remaining matched comparisons were heterogeneous. Isolated advantages appeared for particular logistic-regression/comparator pairs even where the primary all-feature comparison did not establish a gain, while some Covertype small-NN comparisons were unfavorable. Such findings are useful for characterizing the landscape of the method, but they should not be aggregated into a broad “wins over feature selection” claim. The matched windows share seeds, subsets, and evaluation data, and the compact win counts are descriptive summaries of prespecified corrected contrasts rather than new hypothesis tests.

Together, the baseline results suggest that conformal-harm selection is not simply reproducing conventional importance rankings, and on Dry Bean small NN it identifies subsets with a measurable set-efficiency advantage at matched dimensionality. At the same time, the variability across datasets, models, paths, and comparators implies that the method should be evaluated as a task-specific selection objective rather than a universal replacement for conventional feature selection.

## 6.4 Interaction between harmful-feature removal and probability scaling

The interaction with probability scaling provides a second boundary on the contribution. H3 is partially supported descriptively, but the evidence is for sub-additive complementarity rather than general synergy. The clearest case is again the HAR small neural network. With all features, ordinary temperature scaling increased mean APS set size relative to Base, whereas ConfTS reduced it. Under one-shot feature removal, a residual feature benefit remained after ConfTS, and the combined all-Base to selected-ConfTS reduction was larger than either intervention considered in isolation. However, the additive-excess interval was entirely negative. The joint gain was therefore smaller than the sum of the separate feature-removal and ConfTS gains.

This pattern is best described as complementarity in the limited sense that the two interventions are not completely redundant: after scaling with ConfTS, one-shot feature removal still yielded an additional mean efficiency gain. It is not synergy in an additive-interaction sense. The recursive HAR path provides weaker support because its residual feature-gain interval under ConfTS crossed zero even though the additive excess remained negative. The frozen H3 interpretation therefore appropriately emphasizes the one-shot HAR small-NN result and avoids generalizing the interaction beyond the observed settings.

The Dry Bean result provides an informative counterexample. In the primary APS setting, ConfTS used T=1 for the relevant subsets, so there was no scaling benefit to combine with feature removal. The Dry Bean efficiency gain was therefore attributable to selection under the frozen analysis rather than to a favorable interaction with conformal temperature scaling. Covertype similarly showed little or no scaling benefit in several strata. These negative and null cases are important because they show that feature intervention and scaling are not two components of a universally beneficial recipe.

The HAR scaling results also demonstrate why efficiency and valid conformal performance must be separated. For HAR small NN, ConfTS produced smaller sets than Base in the all-feature and selected settings, but coverage remained below the nominal target. The selected one-shot ConfTS mean set size was below one, yet a mean below one can coexist with empty prediction sets and undercoverage and is not, by itself, an improvement. Accordingly, H3 should be interpreted as a statement about efficiency interactions in the frozen data, not about a safe or generally valid scaling strategy.

A further inferential limitation is specific to H3. The mean interaction algebra was independently reconciled from the frozen result rows, but the original Phase 8 script that generated the reported H3 confidence intervals is unavailable. Those intervals are therefore retained as supplied frozen evidence rather than independently regenerated inference. This limitation does not change the direction of the verified mean contrasts, but it constrains how strongly the interaction intervals can be presented as independently audited results.

## 6.5 Subject-level robustness and the HAR reliability problem

The HAR subject analysis separates repeatability of the efficiency effect from validity of the conformal predictor. The two-way seed/subject bootstrap reproduced positive subject-weighted set-size reductions for the small neural network under both one-shot and recursive selection, with intervals excluding zero. This result is valuable because it shows that the small-NN efficiency effect is not merely an artifact of pooling windows without regard to subject structure. In contrast, the logistic-regression subject-size intervals crossed zero, consistent with the weaker primary LR evidence.

The corresponding coverage analysis does not show a reliability improvement. All four subject-weighted coverage-difference intervals—both models and both selection paths—crossed zero. These intervals do not establish equivalence; they simply fail to establish a directional coverage effect. The aggregate conditional metrics also remained mixed. For example, the recursive small-NN path improved SSCV in the reported primary setting, whereas the one-shot path did not, and class-coverage-gap changes were not uniformly favorable. H4 is therefore not generally supported.

This distinction matters because subject-level robustness of an *effect estimate* is not the same as nominal conformal validity. Subject-disjoint splitting prevents the direct leakage that would occur if windows from the same person appeared in both fitting and evaluation partitions, but it does not prove that windows are exchangeable across held-out subjects. Nor does it guarantee that a standard split-conformal calibration argument remains well matched to the grouped and shifted structure of HAR. The observed HAR undercoverage is consequently a property that must remain visible even though the efficiency effect is reproducible under subject-weighted resampling.

The statistical unit must also remain explicit. Each primary HAR subject contrast is based on 100 observed seed-by-held-out-subject cells arising from 20 seeds and 26 unique subject identifiers, and the frozen bootstrap resamples the seed and subject dimensions. The 146,500 subject-condition rows are not 146,500 independent subjects, and overlapping subject appearances across seeds do not create additional independent replications. The appropriate conclusion is therefore narrow: harmful-feature removal yields reproducible subject-weighted efficiency gains for the HAR small neural network, but neither subject-level coverage improvement nor restoration of nominal conformal validity is established.

## 6.6 Numerical behavior and diagnostic limitations

The HAR results are further qualified by substantial numerical-boundary behavior. Across the full frozen grid, 10,006 rows carried raw-boundary flags and 5,115 rows carried scaling-induced flags. All scaling-induced flags occurred under HAR ConfTS. This concentration is important because ConfTS can sharpen probability vectors, and boundary behavior can materially affect adaptive conformal scores and set construction. The diagnostics therefore identify a region of the evaluation in which numerical behavior cannot be ignored.

At the same time, the diagnostics do not establish a causal explanation for HAR undercoverage. Base HAR also contained thousands of raw-boundary flagged rows, including 4,343 under Base, so undercoverage and boundary flags are not unique to ConfTS. The observed co-occurrence of scaling-induced flags and small ConfTS sets is insufficient to show that saturation is the sole, or even dominant, cause of the coverage deficit. Establishing causality would require a dedicated numerical intervention study, which was not part of the frozen Phase 8 design and should not be inferred post hoc from diagnostic frequencies.

The denominators are also crucial. The reported counts are frequencies over a dependent experimental grid whose rows share datasets, seeds, models, subsets, scaling configurations, and score settings. They are not independent failure probabilities at the subject, seed, model, or prediction level. No flagged rows were removed from the analysis, so the primary results preserve the prespecified evaluation population rather than filtering numerically inconvenient cases after seeing the outcomes.

These diagnostics reinforce a broader reporting principle: very small prediction sets should never be interpreted in isolation. In particular, a mean set size below one can arise when some prediction sets are empty, and it is not evidence of improved conformal performance if empirical coverage is inadequate. Efficiency, marginal coverage, conditional behavior, empty-set behavior, and numerical-boundary diagnostics should therefore be presented together whenever aggressive scaling or feature selection produces unusually compact sets.

## 6.7 Implications, limitations, and scope of the method

The main scientific contribution of this study is not a claim that a universal set of harmful features exists. Rather, the evidence shows that feature contributions to adaptive conformal efficiency can diverge from conventional notions of predictive importance, and that exploiting this divergence can improve prediction-set efficiency in some settings. The strongest case is Dry Bean with the small neural network, where the proposed removals improved set efficiency, were competitive with or better than all matched selectors, and did not exhibit the severe coverage problem seen on HAR. The HAR small-NN case shows a different and equally important boundary: efficiency gains can be reproducible while nominal coverage remains unsatisfactory. These two cases together motivate treating conformal efficiency as a distinct feature-selection objective while requiring validity diagnostics to accompany any efficiency claim.

Several limitations constrain the scope of this conclusion. First, the effects are model-dependent and dataset-dependent. The small neural network showed the clearest gains, whereas logistic regression and Covertype provided weaker or mixed evidence. Second, the identities of selected features were only partially stable across seeds. The method can therefore produce a stable aggregate effect without yielding a stable attribution to the same individual variables. Third, robustness was evaluated only over the frozen hyperparameter and sensitivity grid. The study varied the prespecified accuracy-loss allowances, removal counts, alpha values, and RAPS settings, but it does not establish behavior outside that design space, and no post hoc choice among sensitivity cells should be treated as a newly optimized operating point.

The evidence audit also has source limitations. The original H3 interval-generation code is unavailable, so the interaction confidence intervals remain supplied Phase 8 evidence even though their mean algebra was independently verified. Original split-index files and individual unit completion manifests are also unavailable; the audit verifies recorded split identifiers, provenance fields, and exact expected HAR subject membership rather than reconstructing the full historical split-generation process. In addition, per-window prediction sets are unavailable, which prevents reconstruction of some pooled quantities—most notably pooled size quantiles such as `size_p90`—from the subject-level summaries. None of these gaps requires changing the frozen H1–H4 interpretations, but they limit the extent of independent reconstruction that can be claimed.

The HAR setting raises a deeper statistical limitation. Subject-disjoint splitting is an important protection against direct subject leakage, but grouped human-activity data can violate the exchangeability assumptions under which standard split conformal coverage is usually interpreted. The persistent HAR undercoverage means that the current feature-selection procedure should not be viewed as solving distribution shift or grouped-data validity. More generally, the seed-level intervals quantify repeat-run variability conditional on these datasets, models, and frozen data-generating procedures; dependent grid rows, matched windows, overlapping seed pairs, and repeated subject appearances are not additional independent replications from a broader population.

Finally, the empirical scope remains limited to three real datasets and two classifier families. This is sufficient to demonstrate that conformal-harm selection can differ from ordinary importance and can yield meaningful efficiency gains, but not to establish universal behavior across domains, architectures, class counts, feature types, or shift regimes. Practical use should therefore be conditional: evaluate the proposed selection on held-out tuning data, freeze the choice before conformal calibration and test evaluation, and report accuracy, set efficiency, empirical coverage, conditional metrics, and numerical diagnostics together. A smaller set alone is not a valid success criterion.

Future work should test the phenomenon across substantially more diverse datasets and model classes, especially settings with many plausible labels and heterogeneous feature structures. Grouped and shifted data require methods that explicitly respect dependence or distribution shift rather than relying on subject-disjoint splitting alone. Numerical safeguards should be made part of the method itself, with stable probability computations and prespecified responses to saturation or boundary events. Finally, stronger procedures are needed for learning *stable* conformal-harm structures—for example, by distinguishing interchangeable correlated features from consistently harmful feature groups and by quantifying selection uncertainty across resamples. Those advances would be necessary to move from the present evidence for context-dependent efficiency-oriented selection toward a more general theory and practice of feature selection for valid adaptive conformal prediction.
